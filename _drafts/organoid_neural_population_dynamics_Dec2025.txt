Mulitple realizability in brain organoids; let's focus on function
"What I cannot build, I do not understand" Richard Feynman

Early neurophysiological studies of the visual system painted a picture of rigid hierarchy. The Nobel-prize winning model by Hubel and Wiesel is summarized in Fig.1. Beginning with photon detection in the retina, the center-surround retinal ganglion cell output leads to center-surround receptive fields of thalamic neurons onto 'simple' edge-detecting neurons in layer IV of the cortex whose combined inputs then lead to complex feature (e.g. movement or object) detecting neurons in layer II/III of the visual cortex. This heirarchical model depends on precise wiring between cells and microcircuits of the visual system. (Fig.1A)[https://www.columbia.edu/cu/neurotheory/Ken/fm_final/node3.html]

Although systems neuroscience has moved beyond these early models, they are the first models of brain systems taught to most researchers and remain influential today. In the trenches of todays's translational brain organoid neuroscience a prevalent dogma goes something like this: if we can recreate the precise wiring and microcircuitry of brain area X (e.g the visual cortex) in a dish, then we should be able to create a functioning area X as a consequence of that precise structure. 

Tremendous work has been put into identifying organoid protocols that reliably recreate immunohistochemical markers of cortical layers/different molecular subtypes of neurons/neural morphologies closest to the brain structure that the organoid is modeling. Much less work, mostly due to technical limitations, has gone into determining function in brain organoids. Given the enormous resources needed for these projects, researchers have systematically focused on recreating precise structure, implicitly assuming accurate structure is absolutely needed to recreate function. 

Curiously, if modern systems neuroscience is to be believed, this percieved requirement might be overly strict. Over the past few decades, as the ability of neurophysiologists to record electrical activity from more and more neurons in behaving animals has gone up, a consensus is building that a given brain area's function/s can be realized using multiple combinations of molecular and structural parameters. Function may be realized in multiple structural ways!

To understand why structure might not dictate function as strictly as we think, we must revisit David Marr. Working at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in the late 1970s, Marr proposed a framework for understanding information processing systems that remains relevant today. He argued that to understand a system like the visual cortex, one must analyze it at three distinct levels: the computational level (what is the goal?), the algorithmic level (what are the representations and processes?), and the implementation level (the physical hardware). See Fig.2 of [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0896627316310406]

The translational brain organoid field need not be solely focused on the 'implementation' level. That is, on recreating cortical layers, cell types, synaptic and other protein markers etc. Instead, the field should evaluate more the computational capabilities of the brain organoids they create.

Consider the primary visual cortex (V1). Individual neurons here are notoriously variable; their firing patterns fluctuate due to noise, synaptic efficacy, or neuromodulation. Yet, when hundreds of neurons are recorded simultaneously, their collective activity traces out smooth, stable geometric shapes in high-dimensional space, known as **neural manifolds**. 

These manifolds are low-dimensional structures that capture the essential correlations between neurons. While the activity of any single neuron might be noisy, the population signal—the manifold—is robust. It accurately represents stimulus features, such as orientation angle, and transmits this information to downstream areas. Crucially, recent work has shown that these manifolds can be aligned across different individuals. The "implementation" (synaptic weights, exact wiring) differs from mouse to mouse, but the "computation" (the geometry of the manifold) is conserved. Fig. 3 see [https://www.thetransmitter.org/neural-dynamics/neural-manifolds-latest-buzzword-or-pathway-to-understand-the-brain/]

### The Avian Argument

The case for multiple realizability becomes even stronger when we look across species. Consider the mammalian V1 and its avian analogue, the Wulst (or hyperpallium). Anatomically, they are starkly different. The mammalian cortex is a layered sheet; the avian Wulst is organized into nuclear clusters. A histologist looking at the two would see little resemblance.

Yet, at the computational level, they are remarkably similar. Both structures process visual information to extract features like orientation and motion. If we were to record high-dimensional population dynamics in the Wulst, we would likely find neural manifolds topologically similar to those in the mammalian V1, despite the radical difference in physical architecture. Evolution has solved the same computational problem using two very different physical implementations. Fig 4 see [https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Schematics-of-the-layers-subdivisions-cell-types-and-major-connections-of-the-mammalian_fig2_285929394]

### Liberating the Organoid

This insight is critical for the translational potential of brain organoids. If nature allows for multiple realizability—if a bird and a mammal can perform the same visual computations with different hardware—why do we demand that human brain organoids be perfect structural replicas of the fetal cortex to be considered useful?

Multiple realizability suggests a new path forward: **Functional Phenotyping**. Instead of judging an organoid solely by its histology, we should judge it by its manifolds.

Imagine a "Turing test" for brain organoids. We stimulate an organoid (perhaps using optogenetics to mimic retinal input) and record the population dynamics using high-density probes. We then ask: Does the population activity collapse onto a low-dimensional manifold? Is that manifold stable? Does its geometry resemble the manifolds seen in *in vivo* data?

If an organoid generates the correct neural dynamics, it is a valid model of cortical computation, even if its lamination is imperfect or its cell-type ratios are slightly off. Indeed, combining the stochastic variability of organoid cultures, scRNASeq and the appropriate functional assay might lead to a better understanding of the true parameters governing brain function.

### Conclusion

This is not an excuse for sloppy culture work. We must still strive for the highest possible structural fidelity. However, we must recognize that "fidelity" is not a binary metric, where only structural fidelity is the gold standard. 

By embracing the systems neuroscience perspective, we can move the goalposts in a productive way. We can stop asking, "Does this look exactly like a brain?" and start asking, "Does this compute like a brain?"
