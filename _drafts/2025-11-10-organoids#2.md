## Intro

Brain organoids are pretty hot these days, mostly among cell and molecular brain researchers. "Mini-brains in a dish" has almost achieved meme status for these nerds. There are some legitimate reasons for this and some less so. I thought I should put down some of them in writing as 2025 draws to a close.
Although only about 15 years old, the field has grown hugely. The flow-chart below shows my own big-picture map of the field.
{flow-chart}

Though their experimental approaches differ tremendously, people in each of these sub-fields are, in my humble opinion, fundamentally motivated by one major question. "How closely does a brain organoid mimic a similar-sized piece of intact human brain?" Over the last two years of experimental work, I tried to focus on a narrower question:"how closely does a _cortical_ organoid of human origin approach the computational capabilities of a similar-sized piece of _human cortex_? I wish the answer was "really close". Unfortunately, it's more like "not even close!". That said, I believe that even though the path to the ulitmate goal is currently hidden, we might learn many useful secrets of nature if we nevertheless keep at it for a bit.

My own narrower question is a morass of unsolved issues. There is debate starting with the phrase "computational capabilities". Serious arguments have been made, and not fully refuted, regarding the 'computational' nature of the brain.
The brain had been studied for a many centuries before McCulloch and Pitts suggested (1) that it is a machine devoted to logic i.e. computation. Older metaphors compared the brain to a pump, pumping 'animal spirits' around the body (Descartes) (2). More recently, the brain, when controlling behavior, has been compared to a thermostat-like device i.e. mostly engaged in feedback control (perceptual control theory, William Powers / Henry Yin) comprising an heirarchy of control loops.This feedback control metaphor does not invoke internal 'representations' which need to be computed on by brain circuits as assumed by computational cognitive framework.
