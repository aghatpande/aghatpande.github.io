## Intro

Brain organoids are pretty hot these days. The quite misleading phrase "mini-brains in a dish" has almost achieved meme status in current times. There are some legitimate reasons for this and some less so. I thought I should write down my grasp of some aspects of the field as 2025 draws to a close, especially since I spent the last two years recording electrical signals from them.

Brain organoids are ~ half to few mm diameter, three dimensionally organized pieces of approximately spherical brain tissue that begin their journey as a clump of genetically identical pluripotent stem cells. These stem cells can come from embryonic sources (mostly non-human animals) abbreviated ePSCs or from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) that can be generated from almost any sampled tissue of animals (mostly humans). With the right patterning cues they can self-organize into layered, brain region-specific structures (e.g. cortex, hippocampus etc.) that recapitulate many molecular, cellular, and structural features of the developing brain, while obviously missing sensory inputs and motor outputs. The people in the field are quite aware that brain organoids fall short in capturing the complexity and scale of an intact nervous system.

Although only about 15 years old, the field has grown hugely. The flow-chart below shows my own big-picture map of the field.

Though their experimental approaches differ tremendously, people in each of these sub-fields are, in my humble opinion, fundamentally motivated by one overarching question. "How closely does a brain organoid mimic a similar-sized piece of intact human / animal brain?" Over the last two years of experimental work at the University of Pennsylvania, I tried to focus on a slightly different and narrower question:"how closely does a _cortical_ organoid of human origin approach the computational capabilities of a similar-sized piece of _human cortex_? I have to say I am far from any answer. I do wish the answer was "really close". Unfortunately, my semi-educated guess is that we are not that close! That said, I believe we might learn many useful secrets of Nature if we keep at it for a bit.

## Non-computational metaphors

My seemingly narrower question is an obstacle course of unsolved issues. The first stumbling block is the phrase “computational capabilities”. Serious arguments have been made disputing even the “computational” nature of the brain, let alone agreeing on a set of commonly accepted capabilities. Let me briefly describe one such argument.

The brain had been studied for many centuries before McCulloch and Pitts suggested (1) that it could be viewed as a machine devoted to logic — in other words, that it executes algorithms or computations. But the desire to 'interpret' brain function by comparing it to something more understandable, began perhaps with Descartes who compared the brain to a pump circulating “animal spirits” around the body (2). More recently, the brain of awake behaving animals has been compared to an incredibly complex thermostat. There are at least a few different flavors of this control-theoretic metaphor, including classical cybernetics, phylogenetic refinement of feedback control, and the one I’ll briefly describe here, “Perceptual Control Theory”.

Perceptual Control Theory was originally developed by William T. Powers and has been applied to neurophysiological and behavioral data by Henry Yin and others. The theory proposes that the brain is a vast hierarchy of control loops, each engaged in feedback control. Very simply, the job of any given loop / functional area is to keep a particular perceptual variable close to a reference value (analogous to the temperature setting on a thermostat). The reference for a given area is determined by an area “above” it in the hierarchy. Crucially, reference signals are set internally by the system unlike engineered devices like thermostats where the reference signal is set by some external agent.

The overall set of reference values for the animal, therefore, becomes its own needs and wants, spread over multiple timescales and life stages. For example, lack of food in the animal’s environment might cause deviations from some internal metabolic setpoint, which then triggers movement away from the current environment, depending on a higher-level configuration of reference points. This feedback-control metaphor argues that one can explain a great deal of brain function without proposing elaborate algorithms: each loop simply computes the difference between its current perceptual input and a reference, then acts to reduce that error, with those actions themselves feeding back through the environment.

Control-theoretic views like Perceptual Control Theory are attractive because they make brain function look almost “algorithm-free”: just nested error-correcting loops keeping important variables within acceptable ranges. Its also surprisingly effective at explaining some aspects of behavior observed in experimental situations (Yin reference).

At the same time, many of the questions we ask about brain organoids — especially about their “capabilities” — are easier to pose in the language of computation, algorithms, and information processing. In the next section, I’ll focus on the computational metaphor, while keeping in mind that what we call “computation” might itself just be a very elaborate form of feedback control.

## The brain as a computer

The predominant paradigm (common set of assumptions) in the modern neurosciences is to view the brain / nervous system as an evolutionarily cobbled-together, self-organizing, algorithm-running machine. This paradigm is a result of hundreds of years of empirical studies focusing on small and large aspects of nervous system structure-function spanning many different phyla and many different scales (molecular to populations of individuals), not really a theoretical proposal by any individual / small group, though many influential scientists adhering to this paradigm have contributed to its prodominance.
At the very heart of this modern view is an utterly frustating puzzle. We can describe / quantitatively simulate most significant biophysical / molecular / cellular details and connections of some of the simpler nervous systems (e.g. C. elegans and other invertebrate systems) and yet, we, collectively speaking, are unable to predict the behavior of these 'simple' animals! What are we missing...?
